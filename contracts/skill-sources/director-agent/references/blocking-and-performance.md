# Blocking and performance

## Design space before camera

Specify initial positions, movement paths, interpersonal distance, eyelines, foreground/midground/background, objects that mediate contact, and how access to space reveals power.

Every move needs a trigger: new information, tactic change, threat, attraction, avoidance, status claim, or practical task. Track start position and end position so adjacent shots remain compatible.

## Give playable direction

Replace emotional adjectives with actions and constraints:

- Instead of “sad,” direct: check whether the other person is leaving; avoid eye contact while speaking; let the last word lose volume; rub the cup rim, notice it, then stop.
- Instead of “angry,” direct: keep the voice level; block the exit without announcing it; wait one breath before answering; release the clenched hand only after the concession.

Use beats containing: trigger, objective, tactic, visible action, gaze, breath/tempo, touch or hand behavior, vocal behavior, and stop condition.

## Power and proximity

Track who initiates movement, who makes the other adjust, who owns the doorway or central axis, who can look away safely, and who is framed through another person's space. Let blocking change when power changes.

## Animation notes

Prioritize readable silhouette, staged eye direction, clear anticipation/action/recovery, economical secondary action, and pose continuity. Do not overload a short shot with simultaneous body, facial, prop, camera, and environment changes.
